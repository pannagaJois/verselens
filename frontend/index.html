The Sanctuary of Non-Human Presence

Wendell Berry opens with an interior psychological crisis—a nocturnal waking prompted by “despair for the world” and paternal anxiety regarding his children’s destiny. The first movement builds rapid tension through run-on clause accumulation, capturing the modern mind trapped within cyclical anticipation of calamity.

The poem’s turning point occurs with geographical and physical motion: “I go and lie down where the wood drake / rests in his beauty on the water.” Berry does not merely seek refuge in landscape as an abstract scenic relief; he seeks communion with creatures that exist outside the human curse of temporal forethought. The wild creatures do not calculate grief before its arrival. Their stillness confers permission for the speaker to shed his cognitive burden.

In the final couplet, Berry introduces the paradox of “day-blind stars / waiting with their light.” The cosmic permanence of the stars—invisible yet unwavering—mirrors the enduring, silent grace of the ecology itself. Release does not arrive through resolution of the world’s crises, but through immersion in the unhurried present of the natural order.

When despair for the world grows in me

and I wake in the night at the least sound

in fear of what my life and my children’s lives may be,

I go and lie down where the wood drake

rests in his beauty on the water, and the great heron feeds.

I come into the peace of wild things

who do not tax their lives with forethought

of grief. I come into the presence of still water.

And I feel above me the day-blind stars

waiting with their light. For a time

I rest in the grace of the world, and am free.
